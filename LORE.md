# AGR-31 Tenpin

Flavour and employment doctrine. Everything here matches what the weapon actually
does in game.

## The name

**AGR-31** is the customer's designation, issued when the round was brought back
into service. It sits in the same family as the AGR-18 Lynchpin and the AGR-24
Kingpin, and that placement was a procurement decision rather than an engineering
one: the number says "ground attack rocket", and the family says "you already know
how to use this".

**Tenpin** follows the family's naming convention, and whoever chose it was making a
point. The kingpin is the pin you aim at. The tenpin is the one in the back corner
that nothing reaches cleanly, and the way you take it down is not a better line, it
is more balls.

## The problem it was built for, eighty years ago

The Tenpin is older than the Meridian Combine, older than the trading years, and
older than the political arrangement that produced either. It was designed for a war
nobody involved now remembers fighting, against a problem that has not changed:
dispersed light targets, defended, that are not worth a guided weapon each.

The original round was a 90 mm unguided rocket in a steel drum, and it was built to
be cheap in the way that only wartime articles are. The design brief has survived
in the Combine's archive and is three pages long. Most of it is about cost.

It worked, in the sense that things like it always work. It was produced in
quantities that nobody has ever fully accounted for, in a period when production was
the only metric anyone was measuring, and then the war it was made for ended and
several hundred thousand rounds went into depots.

There they sat. Long enough that the state that ordered them stopped existing, and
the depots changed hands twice without anyone taking much of an interest in what was
in them.

## How the Combine came to own it

When the Combine was founded as a joint Boscali and Primevan venture, it acquired
plant, tooling and inventory in the untidy way that industrial mergers do. The
Tenpin came with a factory the Combine wanted for other reasons. The rounds came with
the depots attached to it.

For most of the trading years this was a liability on the books. Old ordnance is
expensive to store and more expensive to dispose of, and there was no market for an
unguided rocket in a decade when everything was going precision. The Combine
periodically costed the demilitarisation and periodically decided to leave it another
year.

## Why it came back

The war did what wars do to procurement arithmetic.

Precision weapons are slow to build and the bottleneck is not the airframe, it is the
seeker. Both belligerents discovered within the first year that they could not
produce guided munitions at the rate they were expending them, and that no amount of
money fixes a supply chain in less than about three years.

At the same time a capability gap opened that nobody had planned for. Short range air
defence proliferated faster than anything else on the battlefield, because it is
cheap and it works, and the standard answer to it was a guided round costing more
than the launcher it was destroying. The exchange rate was upside down and everyone
could see it.

The Combine's contribution was to point out that it was sitting on several hundred
thousand rockets that already existed.

**The argument that sold it** was not that the Tenpin was good. It was that a
saturation weapon does not need a seeker, that the inventory was already paid for
eighty years ago, and that the only thing standing between a depot and a pylon was a
refurbishment line. Both customers bought it. Neither has publicly acknowledged
buying it from the same firm.

## What the Combine changed

The rocket body is essentially the original. It is the same 90 mm, two metre round
with the same fin arrangement, because it was correct the first time and requalifying
a new one would have defeated the entire point of the exercise.

Everything around it is new:

- **A new motor**, filling the original's case. Modern propellant roughly doubles the
  reach of the same envelope. This is where the 15 to 20 km comes from, and it is the
  single change that made the round relevant again.
- **An inertial seeker**, which is not guidance in any sense the word usually carries.
  It cannot see the target and does not try to. It holds the round to the ballistic
  solution it was launched on, which is the difference between a salvo that lands in a
  pattern and one that lands in a county.
- **A new pod.** The original steel drum was replaced with a chamfered hexagonal
  composite shroud, in seven and nineteen tube variants. It is lighter, it is cheaper
  to make than the drum was, and the flats give a mounting face the old cylinder never
  had.
- **Hardening.** The rounds are built to survive being shot at, in the specific and
  limited sense that a defence has to commit more than one interceptor to be confident
  of a kill. Against a magazine, that is worth more than speed.

What was deliberately not changed: the warhead is small, the round is not accurate on
its own, and neither of those is going to be fixed. They are the reason the thing is
affordable.

## Doctrine

**The Tenpin is a counter-SHORAD weapon and it is not a substitute for a Kingpin.**
It carries a fraction of the Kingpin's penetration and half its per-round kill
probability. Against a bunker or an armoured vehicle it is the wrong article.
Against a battery, it works precisely because there are forty two of it.

**Range is the whole point.** Fifteen to twenty kilometres puts the launching
aircraft outside the engagement envelope of everything the weapon is designed to
destroy. A crew that closes to see the target better has given away the only
advantage the round has.

**Fire the whole load.** A partial salvo is the worst way to use it: enough rounds to
alert the site, not enough to overwhelm it. Doctrine is a full ripple from every pod,
which puts the light load down in a little over three seconds and the heavy load in
about nine.

**Saturation is a magazine argument, not a damage argument.** The round is not hard
to shoot down. It is hard to shoot down forty two of, and a defence that engages
correctly still runs out of interceptors before it runs out of targets. Any tactic
that lets the defence engage the salvo in sequence rather than all at once gives back
the entire advantage.

**Altitude is free range.** The round inherits the aircraft's velocity and thin air
costs it less, so a high fast launch reaches nearly twice as far as a low slow one.
Crews are expected to know this. The reference figures are quoted at 1500 m.

## Carriage

The pods were qualified on the A-19 first, and that is the aircraft they were bought
for. They fit the CAS pylons of several other airframes, including two helicopters
and two trainers, for the unromantic reason that those pylons were already cut for the
AGR-24.

**Qualified is not the same as suited.** The helicopters can carry it and it is a poor
weapon from them: a hovering launch inherits almost no velocity, cannot loft, and
throws away most of the standoff the round exists to provide. Crews who do it anyway
report that the aiming solution feels vague, and they are not wrong. This is a known
limitation of firing an artillery weapon from a platform that cannot behave like
artillery.

## Counters

- **Engage it in sequence.** The salvo's whole advantage is arriving at once. Anything
  that spreads arrival out, terrain, range, a partial launch, hands the defence its
  engagement cycle back.
- **Hit the aircraft.** A loaded A-19 is carrying most of a tonne of rocket at
  fifteen kilometres and has to hold a firing solution to release. It is a much easier
  target than the rounds are.
- **Disperse.** The weapon is sized against a battery sitting in a footprint. A site
  spread wider than the pattern absorbs a full salvo and keeps working.
- **Do not spend interceptors on it.** The correct answer to a Tenpin salvo, and the
  hardest one to give under fire, is usually to let it land.

## Service notes

The refurbishment line has been running for two years and the failures on record are
all of one kind: they are eighty year old rounds.

- Motor cases from certain depot lots are rejected at a rate the Combine describes as
  "within expectation" and its customers describe in other terms.
- Fin assemblies that have been stored folded for four decades do not always deploy
  cleanly. The current pods carry the rounds with fins extended for this reason, which
  is why the tubes are larger than the round needs.
- There is no lot traceability for anything manufactured before the merger. The
  Combine has been asked to provide it and has explained, patiently and more than
  once, that the records were destroyed by the war the rockets were built for.
